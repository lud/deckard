# Presentation craft — distilled advice

Distilled guidance from the presentation-craft canon, for helping the user
convey meaning. Each rule names its source; the evidence-based ones (Mayer,
Alley) rest on controlled studies rather than taste. Use it in conversation:
when a draft breaks a rule, say which rule and why it exists, propose the
fix, and let the user decide — their talk, their call.

## The narrative — ideate and plan

These rules assume a talk arguing one idea. Plenty of legitimate decks do
not: quarterly results, a multi-project status review, a training catalogue
are naturally a **sequence of self-contained sections**, and that shape is
fine. There, apply the rules *within* each section where they help — lead
with the answer suits reporting best of all — sprinkle story where one
genuinely exists, and let the throughline be as light as "here is where we
stand". A narrative forced onto a report reads as spin; a bit of
storytelling always helps, but only where the material honestly carries it.

- **One throughline.** A talk is one connected idea traceable through every
  part; a topic that does not attach to it belongs in the parking lot.
  (Chris Anderson, *TED Talks*.)
- **Contrast what is with what could be.** Alternate between the current
  state and the better future the talk points at — the gap is what creates
  engagement, and the ending lands on the better future. (Nancy Duarte,
  *Resonate*.)
- **Lead with the answer.** State the conclusion first, then support it in
  groups that neither overlap nor leave gaps. Audiences retain structure,
  not sequence. (Barbara Minto, *The Pyramid Principle*.)
- **Make the takeaway sticky.** Test it against: Simple, Unexpected,
  Concrete, Credible, Emotional, carried by a Story. (Chip & Dan Heath,
  *Made to Stick*.)
- **Stories beat assertions.** Narrative is the most reliable carrier of
  meaning — wrap key points in a concrete story with a person in it rather
  than stating them cold. (David JP Phillips, "The magical science of
  storytelling", TEDxStockholm 2017; Carmine Gallo, *Talk Like TED*.)
- **Shorter is stronger.** A constrained length forces the discipline that
  makes ideas land — TED caps talks at 18 minutes on purpose. (Carmine
  Gallo, *Talk Like TED*.)

## The slides — plan and media

- **One message per slide.** Working memory is small; a slide carrying
  several points delivers none of them. Split rather than shrink — this is
  what companion slides are for. (David JP Phillips, "How to avoid death By
  PowerPoint", TEDxStockholmSalon 2014; Richard Mayer's segmenting
  principle.)
- **Title as assertion, body as evidence.** Write the slide title as a full
  sentence stating the slide's claim, and support it with visual evidence
  rather than a topic word plus bullets — measured comprehension gains.
  (Michael Alley, assertion-evidence structure.)
- **Words plus visuals beat words alone.** People demonstrably learn better
  from both together. (Mayer, multimedia principle.)
- **The audience reads or listens, never both.** On-screen sentences that
  duplicate the spoken word suppress retention of both. Slides carry
  keywords and visuals; the full spoken sentences belong in `deck: Notes`.
  (Mayer, redundancy principle — also the centerpiece of Phillips' talk.)
- **Cut everything that does not serve the message.** Decoration, filler
  stock images, a logo on every slide: extraneous material measurably harms
  learning. (Mayer, coherence principle; Garr Reynolds, *Presentation
  Zen*.)
- **Size signals importance; contrast steers attention.** Make the most
  important element the biggest, and let contrast take the eye where the
  argument is — the audience should never have to search a slide. (Phillips
  2014; Mayer, signaling principle.)
- **At most about six objects per slide.** Past that, the cost of visually
  processing the slide grows out of proportion. (Phillips 2014.)
- **Bullets fragment reasoning.** A hierarchy of fragments hides how claims
  relate; prefer an assertion with evidence, a diagram that shows the
  relations, or prose in the notes. (Edward Tufte, *The Cognitive Style of
  PowerPoint*.)
- **A slide is not a document.** Content that must be *read* belongs in a
  handout or appendix — dense, skimmable pages are a different genre with
  different rules. (Nancy Duarte, *Slidedocs*; Tufte.)

## The media — choosing what to show

- **Visuals prove, text states.** Prefer media that is *evidence* for the
  slide's assertion — the real capture, the actual output, a diagram of the
  true relations — over generic illustration. (Alley; the pipeline's
  never-fabricate rule is the same idea.)
- **Put words next to what they describe.** Labels beside the relevant part
  of an image beat legends and far-away captions. (Mayer, spatial
  contiguity principle.)
- **A dark background keeps focus on the speaker** — worth proposing when
  the speaker, not the screen, carries the substance. (Phillips 2014.)
